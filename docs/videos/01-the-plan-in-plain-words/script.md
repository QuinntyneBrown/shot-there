# 01 · The plan, in plain words

Imagine a map of every place in Toronto where you can take photos: the rental studios, the parks, the bridges, the big glass conservatory. Now imagine one person went to every single one, shot the same test in every room, measured the same things, and wrote down what it really costs for two hours. That map is ShotThere. It's free to read, and nothing is on it unless it has been shot there.

So how does a free map pay the bills? This video explains the business plan in plain words, as of October 2026, including what the next three years might look like.

## The problem

About four thousand six hundred photographers work in Toronto. There are hundreds of studios you can rent by the hour. And there are zero reviews that test them all the same way.

Today, you pick a studio from the owner's own photos and a star rating. You find out about the low ceiling, the tiny elevator and the mandatory cleaning fee when you're standing there with the cases. ShotThere fixes that. Every studio gets the same test shot, the same measurements and the same all-in price, so you can line up any two and see which one is right for your shoot.

## One person, four hats

ShotThere is one person. That works because the founder wears four hats. A photographer, who does every review. A software architect, who builds the site. A salesperson, who signs the sponsors and the licences. And a communicator, who hosts the channel.

And AI carries about half of each hat. Coding agents build the site from the written requirements. AI drafts the scripts, the captions, the chapters and the search pages from the measurement sheet. But there's one rule. AI never visits a room, never measures it and never scores it. The founder is in every room, every time.

## Four jars

Think of the money as four jars. Reading the site stays free, and no jar can buy a score or a spot on the list.

The first jar is the channel. Every review is an episode on YouTube. Ads pay a little. Sponsors pay more, and a sponsor is never a place being reviewed. The gear in the standard test kit carries affiliate links. And members pay five dollars a month for early access, the raw test files and a monthly question-and-answer.

The second jar is the camera. Every review shoot is a real session with a paying client, so a headshot client pays for the day the studio gets tested. After a review is published, a studio can buy photos of its own room, at one fixed price that is the same for every studio and shown on the page. And small groups can book a workshop and shoot a reviewed studio with the founder.

The third jar is the maps. Every outdoor spot gets an illustrated shoot map, with numbered spots, the best light and the lens to bring, for fifteen dollars. Once a year there's a Toronto studio report with every measurement in it. And brands can hire the founder to scout a location using all of that data.

The fourth jar is the computer. The founder builds software, so the whole ShotThere method, the site, the test kit and the scoring, can be rented by a photographer in another city for about eight hundred dollars a month. The measured data can be licensed to booking apps. And a studio can pay nineteen dollars a month to show its own measurements on its own website.

The jars feed each other. A review makes an episode. The episode brings viewers. Viewers book sessions and buy maps. Every session fills the next gallery, and every gallery makes the comparison tool better. And a better comparison tool is exactly what another city wants to rent.

## Year by year

The order matters.

In year one, the camera pays the bills while the map fills up and the channel grows. The founder works on ShotThere about half the week, and the plan says about forty-seven thousand dollars.

In year two, the channel and the maps catch up, the first other city signs on, and the plan says about one hundred and seventy-four thousand.

In year three, the channel, the maps and the computer are more than two thirds of roughly three hundred and forty-five thousand dollars. That matters, because those jars don't need the founder's hours. A photo session does.

Costs stay small: about seventeen thousand dollars in year one. And the careful version of the plan? Cut every number in half.

## The first ninety days

Everything points at one date: launch day, January fifteenth, 2027, right when everyone wants a new headshot for the new year.

Before then, two studios a week get shot, measured and filmed, each with a paying client wherever possible. The site gets built from the requirements, designs and mocks that already exist. By launch day, ten studios, five outdoor spots and ten episodes are live.

After ninety days, the plan checks six targets: fifteen studios live, two thousand subscribers, one hundred thousand views, one thousand email signups, one sponsor signed and one workshop sold out. Miss two or more, and some jars get dropped.

## The rules

The biggest risk isn't money. It's trust. A review site that gets paid by the places it reviews stops being a review site.

So ShotThere has a list of things the founder never does. Never sell a score or a position. Never let a studio see a review early, except to check the facts. Never take a sponsor from a place under review. Never let AI measure or score a room. Never list a place that hasn't been shot there. And never hide a bad finding.

Every price and every permit carries the date it was checked, and anything older than six months gets a flag on the page. The map stays honest, or it isn't worth anything.

## Things to remember

- ShotThere is free to read, and nothing is listed unless it has been shot there.
- One founder, four hats, and AI carrying half of each. AI never measures and never scores.
- The money comes from four jars: the channel, the camera, the maps and the computer.
- The camera pays first. The channel, the maps and the computer pay most by year three.
- Trust is the product. No jar buys a score.
